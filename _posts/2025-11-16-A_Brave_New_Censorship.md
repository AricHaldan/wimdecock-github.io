title: "A Brave New Censorship"
date: 2025-11-16
---

The internet has long been a space which has lacked strong regulations and its early days are even sometimes likened to the wild west, but more and more governments are setting out to create new rules for how the internet should work. There are, of course, plenty of reasons for these new rules, since the internet has become a very large part of all of our lives. It has become both a great source of information, recreational content and now increasingly of disinformation. However, within this new surge of regulations, we also risk overextending the government’s reach into what are essentially private matters and risk endangering our freedom of information and our privacy. Many of the new laws that were voted on over the previous decade are now slowly coming into effect. As part of this, on the 25th of July, the UK rolled out new measures to ensure the safety of children online through requiring all “user to user” and online search services to check for the user’s age. This regulatory requirement of age assurance forces UK citizens to verify their age through providing facial scans and/or government ID to third party guarantors or to the sites themselves. And while the goal of the measure might be laudable, I believe these kinds of measures are not only misguided, they are also overly invasive and ineffective. What’s worse is that, even though these measures have kicked up a lot of protest and caused a petition to remove them to get a million signatures in a week’s time, there seems to be no desire of politicians to listen to these critiques. They are simply shoved aside and deemed unworthy of even a parliamentary discussion. Especially with their implementation of their child-safety duties, I find the UK to increasingly be a cautionary tale on what we need to avoid. Because all across the world, we see similar movements to restrict what we can see, what we can buy and what we can engage with and if we don’t do anything, then no amount of VPN’s will give us back the freedom of the internet we have become so accustomed to. 

Even from the start, their stated purpose, to safeguard children from harmful content through governmental regulation, is a misguided view on what the government’s role is. The content they seek to regulate, including pornographic or violent material, is not necessarily harmful to a child. It is a type of content which could potentially disrupt their mental development, but they are not causing any direct harm to any child. And when it comes to determining what is harmful, what is positive and what is permissible for a child’s mental and social development, these laws are encroaching upon the territory of parenting, one of the most private matters in our lives. In all these cases, it should be up to the parents to decide how they want to raise their child and in truth, no government should have any say in the parent’s decisions. This seems to me to be such a simple fact that nevertheless gets overlooked by all politicians seeking to enact child safety laws. If the government wishes to provide more effective tools to parents to regulate their child’s online activities, it would have none of the problems I will talk about in the rest of this article. But by abandoning the principle of parents being the ones who should decide how their children are raised, we are left with an almost dystopian system of laws that seeks to regulate even our most private moments. And the inane desire to force a one size fits all solution on our children also forces adults to go through unnecessary hoops and unacceptable risks to their privacy in order to access the content they have every right to access.

One of the main problems of these laws and the UK’s implementation of the Online Safety Act of 2023 is that the wording is highly subjective, vague and all in all too encompassing. There are terms thrown around such as “highly effective”, "harmful”, “age-appropriate” and “proportional” that all sound very good, but that leave every aspect of this law open to interpretation . What constitutes a highly effective age assurance method ? What type of content is harmful to children and how should that harm be defined ? When is something age-appropriate ? How far can these measures go to be proportional to the “risks” ? Effectively, the details of this implementation are all left to Ofcom, the UK's regulator for communications services. Effectively, the executive government can now fully control the scope and nature of this law, since the legislature has failed to implement limits within its wording of these statutes. It should fall to the legislature, who can freely, and extensively discuss the nuances of these problems to determine what these laws can be used for and what they cannot be used for. That is the role of parliament within a separation of powers and it prevents abuse. Yet the vagueness of these statutes gives the executive untold discretionary powers over what is considered acceptable for our children to see as well as determine what measures we, as adults, need to go through to prove we are not children. This is quite simply too much. After all, what’s to stop any future government from finding the political talking points of their opponents ‘harmful to the youth’ ? What’s to stop them from demanding unreasonable methods of age-verification ? And what’s to stop companies from taking the easy way out and using these vague wordings of “highly effective” and “proportional” to completely disregard privacy concerns in their implementation ?

Even with the current implementation, resulting from Ofcom’s current interpretation of these terms, we already have a greatly increased risk of privacy concerns, which I personally would find unacceptable. As a result of these changes we now have several high profile sites such as Spotify, Reddit, Discord and the app formerly known as Twitter which require or will soon require facial scans as part of their age-verification. This is a highly intrusive measure and forces a large number of adults to either circumvent the measures through a VPN or submit highly personal data to companies whose trustworthiness is questionable at best. While the law might require immediate deletion of these data, there is no guarantee that this will actually take place within all companies requiring these measures and all third party software that provides age-verification services. As a result, these kinds of policies open up everyday citizens to abuse by corporate scams and highly increases the risk of data breaches with highly personal data. It is ludicrous that the UK is now enforcing laws demanding the release of personal data which digital safety programs would strongly advise you not to share with strangers, especially in an age of deepfakes and AI where this kind of data will be even easier to exploit for identity theft or doxxing. Even the Wikimedia Foundation, which runs Wikipedia, has argued in court that this law undermines the privacy and safety of their end-users and volunteers and has warned that they might need to shut down their services in the UK if push comes to shove. Other sites like Imgur have already geoblocked the UK because of the difficulty of meeting this new regulatory burden. It is no wonder that this has caused the UK to see an unprecedented rise in VPN usage almost immediately. It goes against every rational caution to freely give away one’s facial data to shady, unknown age-verification providers, yet that is the reality that the UK now lives in. And if we aren’t careful, the same could be true for any of us within the near-future.
These paternalistic and overly vague regulations put an undue burden on our society and on the internet as it exists and it will lead to far more self-censorship than it has any intention to do. Forcing every adult to prove they are an adult creates unacceptable privacy risks for them, keeping any sane adult at least partially within the scope of this law. As such, it will restrict people’s access to information and recreational content as they have to choose between online safety and the right to be treated as an adult. And the failure to define what constitutes harmful content to a child or what constitutes actual harm done leaves it open to so much interpretation that nothing is off the table. Almost everyone has different ideas for what is bad and what is good for a child’s development and it can be perfectly argued that a lot of aspects can be both vital to development, yet detrimental when overexposed. Politics and history are a perfect example of this, where learning about it is definitely important for our children to grow up into well-rounded adults, yet it is also rife with violence, which could cause desensitization. If these laws force companies to assess risks themselves and handle “proportionally”, then it is more than unlikely that they will choose to avoid risk altogether and hide any content which could in any way be interpreted to be harmful. This broadens the scope of what constitutes adult content beyond anything reasonable and will lead to a new age of censorship as companies fear being held responsible for any child growing up with problems. All of this is the result of the legislature failing to define these risks or even what should be seen as harmful. I can only hope that the European Union, in its implementation of the Digital Services Act of 2022, learns from this absolute aberration of legal principles and sets out to establish a better defined legal framework for these child-safety measures. Or better yet, that they abandon these ideas of making the internet completely safe for children and instead focus on teaching parents about the tools they already have to keep their children safe and help establish new, more effective tools for parents to supervise and restrict their child’s online activities based on their own parental values.

Especially since these laws aren’t even effective at preventing the harm they set out to eliminate. The tools used to scan content for being potentially harmful are far from perfect, leading to both false negatives and false positives, the tools used to verify age can be tricked with game characters and the whole law can be circumvented with VPN’s. There are numerous ways for children to both inadvertently and purposefully access the very content the UK government is seeking to shelter them from. And since no child has ever complained about having unrestricted access to the internet, you can be sure that they will find increasingly creative ways to do exactly that. Even if there are currently reports that fewer children are able to access certain adult sites, it should be noted that new ways of accessing this type of content will be increasingly hard to detect. So, after all is said and done, parental oversight is still the only way to actually protect children. And it is also the only solution to the problem that adult content establishes, which keeps in mind the situation and mental state of every child individually and the will and opinions of the parents themselves. I think it is about time we once again rely on parents to raise their children, rather than relying on the government of all things to raise our children for us.The problem of certain children having unfettered access to the internet is not one that is up to the state to solve. It is a problem we should deal with ourselves, in private, as both individuals and as parents.
